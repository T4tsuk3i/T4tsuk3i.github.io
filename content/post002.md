Over the years on Windows, I never really questioned anything. Starting all the way back with Windows 98, which was my first Windows PC. It was an old PC my mother had gotten from her office. My fascination with PCs in general started pretty early. I took that PC apart a couple of times, although I barely knew what I was looking at. This was back when I couldn't afford an internet connection or even had a phone. That would come another 5 years later, and another 10 years before I actually built my own setup from scratch.

I loved this little, oh sorry, huge beige box with everything hidden inside. Its CD drive, its floppy disk drive and whatever the heck was screaming inside the case. I would later realise this was the CPU fan.

So no, I never questioned Windows when it came pre-installed. It was free and I could do everything in it. Paint, type stuff, install programs by stealing a bit of internet from my mother's phone or, more plausibly, get CDs from my older sister's place to try whatever new things I could find.

I tried everything from games to software to questionable movie CDs. It was also around that time that I started getting interested in movies, mostly Hollywood. That first Smurfs movie in the theatre had lit a spark that wouldn't stop burning. Well, now I had a monitor and some pretty bad speakers, but it was okay. This was my first time being able to watch movies without having to pay every single time I wanted to rewatch them.

Oh, I loved that PC. All through its Windows 98 days, its Windows 2000 days and all the way to XP, where it finally decided to die on me.

No reason as to why. Just death.

That was probably the first time I decided to actually take apart the PC. I was so new to it that I had to scramble around looking for a few screwdriver sets just to find the right one. But I did it. I took it apart and everything fascinated me. The dusty fan, the green sticks of RAM, the hard drive and the power supply. I remember the system having 4GB of DDR2 RAM and a 250GB hard drive. There was even a floppy disk stuck inside the drive. I didn't bother reading what was on it. I just took everything apart.

I disconnected stuff, put it back together and tried powering it on. Nothing.

Again, I went to my sister's place, found a hard drive under a pile of CDs, disconnected the one in my PC and connected this one. And voila! It booted up. Obviously an empty drive would just POST and nothing else, but at least something was happening.

I was back at the same place the system was before I replaced the hard drive, so I wasn't really sure whether I had done anything right or wrong.

My cousins fared a bit better than me since they had a PC early on with internet access. So I went over and connected to the internet for the very first time. It was fun but also a little scary. I did not want to break anything. I started looking around and eventually found out what I was seeing was a BIOS. Then what it was, what it did, what an OS was, how one could get their hands on one and how one would install it.

So going back to that pile of CDs, I found a Windows Vista installation disc. I didn't even know if my system could run it, but I had to give it a try.

I made all the notes I could about the installation process, inserted the CD and started it. It was probably the most gruelling hour of my life. And yeah, it actually took an hour because power wasn't exactly a certain thing back then. We didn't have a UPS, so every time the power went off, I thought something had gone wrong and started all over again.

I think I got it on my fourth try.

But when it finally worked, it was exhilarating. This old system looked new again. I had installed an operating system all by myself, without really knowing what I was doing in the first place.

That was a bit into my journey with Windows. There is a lot more to tell, but for the love of Windows part, I think this should be enough.

---

So yeah, fast-forward to the big ol' 2026. Around July, I was sitting there watching Microsoft Paint Slop at every inch of the system. There were things I never used to question, like why my system was using so much RAM or why there were so many things running in the background. But for some reason, all those questions were screaming at me right now.

I decided to take a look and, for the first time, I genuinely had no idea why my system felt so slow. Something was using resources, but my PC just told me it was a Windows process and nothing else. Mind you, this was not a slow PC.

It was my first custom PC. Ryzen 5 5500 (poor choice, but it was cheap), MSI A520A Pro, 32GB of DDR4 3200MHz RAM, a Radeon RX 7600 8GB and my Samsung Odyssey 24-inch 120Hz monitor. It was a pretty good system, but for some reason it felt like a heavy brick. Everything was running, nothing was particularly broken, but I just wasn't happy with how it felt.

Over the years, I had watched a lot of Windows-to-Linux challenges and I was no stranger to the little penguin. I was a frequent Kali and Parrot OS user, with the occasional experiments with CachyOS. But what actually got me into the Linux train was Linus. The real one, not the Tech Tips one.

He made the Linux kernel and he was using Fedora. I was a developer looking for a bit of stability, but also a bit more freedom. So I picked Fedora.

There is something I find particularly funny about how Linux started. On August 25, 1991, Linus Torvalds posted a message on the comp.os.minix newsgroup announcing his little operating system project. One of the most famous lines from that announcement was:

> "I'm doing a (free) operating system (just a hobby, won't be big and professional like gnu) for 386(486) AT clones."

Just a hobby.

I mean, imagine starting something as a hobby project and watching it eventually become one of the most important pieces of software in the world. Linux now runs on everything from servers and supercomputers to phones and all sorts of embedded devices. And here I am, decades later, installing Fedora because the guy who started it all was using it.

I installed it, spent days experimenting with different desktop environments and finally settled on GNOME. I know why, because I can make it look like a Mac without much effort. Don't judge me.

The transition to Fedora wasn't particularly hard. All I had to do was get the guts to wipe my Windows installation, with Destiny 2 installed on it and over 3800 hours of playtime. That part was painful, especially because Bungie hates Linux and I couldn't just continue playing the game there.

Gaming was a pretty big part of my PC usage too. I played most of my games on my desktop, so leaving Windows meant figuring out what I was going to do about that. I wasn't exactly ready to give up Destiny 2, especially with all those hours invested in it.

Thankfully, I had another option.

I had a Lenovo IdeaPad Gaming 3 that I had bought for my first degree. It had an Intel i5 11300H and an RTX 3050 with 4GB of VRAM. Nothing extraordinary, but good enough for what I needed. I had mostly been using it for my teaching work. I'm a skill development trainer, so this laptop had been with me for quite a while and had already seen its fair share of classes and other work.

So I decided to just install Destiny 2 on it and keep gaming there.

And that's pretty much how my gaming setup ended up being split between two machines. My desktop became my Fedora machine, where I did most of my development, experiments and everything else, while the laptop became my Destiny 2 machine.

Initially, it felt a little inconvenient. Every time my friends needed me for a raid, I'd have to connect my mouse and keyboard from my desktop to the laptop. Then switch my headphones over as well. It was a bit of a process, especially when I had to keep switching between the two systems.

But after a while, it didn't feel that horrible anymore. The laptop was already something I used regularly for teaching, so having Destiny 2 on it wasn't really a big deal. I just had to get used to the whole arrangement.

I actually came across a few stories from people who had made the permanent switch to Linux while looking into all this. One that caught my attention was from a user who had been using Windows 2000 before finally moving to Fedora in 2005. They described wiping Windows off their machine "cold turkey" and never looking back.

Another user talked about starting with Ubuntu through the WUBI installer, initially just experimenting with it, but eventually finding themselves using Linux as their default operating system. They ended up abandoning Windows as their primary OS for good.

I find these stories interesting because everyone seems to have a different reason for making the switch. Some people get tired of Windows, some want better development environments, some want more control over their machines, and some just stumble into Linux and never really feel the need to go back.

I guess I'm somewhere in between.

And honestly, I think I'm pretty content with what I'm running right now. My desktop handles pretty much everything I want to do on Linux, and the laptop takes care of the one game I really didn't want to leave behind. It's not the most elegant setup, but it works.

I started installing Fedora around 11 PM on a Saturday and by 1 AM on Sunday I was sitting in front of my new system. And I was still fascinated by it. My system resource usage seemed to settle around 7GB for most of the things I was doing, GNOME looked pretty much the way I wanted it to, I had automatic wallpapers changing throughout the day and I was finally getting to play around with an operating system rather than just use it.

Then I heard about the built-in Podman setup. I started experimenting with containers, got an \*arr stack running, set up Jellyfin and eventually heard about Distrobox.

Now this was interesting.

At the time, I was doing quite a bit of development work, especially on a project called Sunrise. Initially, I was doing everything directly on my PC. Installing GCC, getting all the dependencies, configuring build tools and basically putting everything the project needed on my actual operating system.

That was getting a bit ugly.

I didn't really want my main system filled with development tools and dependencies for every project I worked on. So I tried Distrobox. I created a Fedora environment, installed all my build tools inside it and started using that as my development environment.

And that was great.

I could keep all my development stuff inside the container, build my binaries there and still use everything normally on my desktop. I could experiment with different environments without having to worry too much about messing up my actual installation. If something broke, well, I could just mess around with the container instead of having to fix my entire system.

I started spending more and more time in the terminal, learning how things worked, figuring out build systems, dependencies and all the little things that Windows had previously hidden away from me.

There is another Linus Torvalds quote that I think fits this part of my journey quite well:

> "Talk is cheap, show me the code."

He said this during an interview when discussing how he deals with criticism and technical discussions. And I think that's something I started appreciating more after moving to Linux. There is only so much you can read and watch. At some point, you just have to sit down, write something, build it and see what happens.

And Sunrise actually gave me one more thing on Linux. Destiny 2 on Linux without the internet. It was amazing. The game that made me hesitate about switching in the first place was now something I could experiment with through my own project. That was a pretty nice feeling.

But then I went down another rabbit hole.

Local AI.

I had been interested in AI for a while, and with all the new models coming out, I wanted to see how much I could actually run on my own hardware. I wasn't particularly interested in just opening another website and chatting with a model. I wanted to run these things myself.

And well, I had an RX 7600 with 8GB of VRAM. Not exactly the most powerful hardware for running local models, but I wanted to see what I could get out of it.

I started experimenting with llama.cpp, GGUF models, quantisation and all the different ways to squeeze larger models into my little 8GB VRAM limit. Eventually, I got into the Qwen models and started experimenting with quantised 9B models.

Running a model locally for the first time was a pretty interesting experience. There was something satisfying about sending a prompt and getting a response from something running entirely on my own PC. No API, no cloud service, no subscription. Just my hardware doing the work.

Of course, I wasn't going to stop there.

I started thinking about making my own AI assistant. Not just another chatbot where I type something and get a response, but an actual assistant that could do things. I wanted it to have memory, tools, the ability to interact with my projects and eventually handle more complicated tasks on its own.

So I started experimenting with different models, trying smaller models to see how much reasoning capability I could get out of them, comparing quantisation formats and figuring out what worked best with my hardware.

I had Qwen running locally, tried different variants, experimented with smaller models and started looking at how I could connect all of this together. The idea was to build something that could use tools, understand what I was working on and actually help me get things done.

I also came across an older interview with Linus where he talked about why people contribute to free software. One thing he said was:

> "Most of the good programmers do programming not because they expect to get paid or get adulation by the public, but because it is fun to program."

And I think that describes a lot of what I'm doing here. Nobody asked me to build an AI assistant that runs on an 8GB GPU. There isn't some commercial reason behind it either. I just want to see if I can do it.

I want to build something that is sophisticated enough to function as my own personal AI system, something that can work with tools, understand context, interact with my development environment and eventually carry out more complicated tasks.

I'm still working on it, and I have a lot more to figure out. But the goal is to make this as sophisticated as I possibly can with the hardware I have. An entire local AI assistant running on a GPU with just 8GB of VRAM. Is it a bit ridiculous? Probably. Am I going to try anyway? Absolutely.

And honestly, I think this is where Linux really started to make sense to me. I could run my models, experiment with inference engines, manage different environments and build the entire thing without having to depend on some external service for every little thing.

So yeah, that's pretty much where I am right now.

From taking apart an old beige PC because I had no idea why it wasn't working, to installing Windows Vista from a random CD, to eventually wiping Windows and moving to Fedora, setting up my own development environments, running my own services and trying to build an AI assistant locally.

I don't think I ever really stopped being that kid who wanted to know what was inside the computer. I just have a lot more things I can break now.

And honestly, I'm pretty happy with where things have ended up. Fedora on my desktop, Windows and Destiny 2 on my laptop, and a bunch of experiments running in between.

At least for now, I'm content with this setup. Though knowing me, that probably won't last forever.
